Calculus is two buttons. Derivative asks "how fast is this changing right now?" Integral asks "how much has piled up in total?"

They undo each other, like × and ÷. Slide the handle: two dots on a curve squeeze together until the line joining them stops being a shortcut and becomes the slope at one point.

1 — The whole thing in two sentences

One asks how steep.
The other asks how much.

Derivative

Steepness at a single point

Look at any curve. Zoom in far enough on one spot and every curve looks like a straight line. The derivative is the slope of that line.

Real name for it in daily life: rate. Speed, wage per hour, degrees per minute, rupees per litre — all derivatives.

dydx = riserun, with the run shrunk to nothing
Integral

Total piled up over a stretch

Now look at the space trapped under that curve. The integral is that area — found by slicing it into thin strips and adding them.

Real name in daily life: total. Distance travelled, money earned, water used, calories eaten.

∫ f(x) dx = add up (height × tiny width), forever

Speedometer → odometer.
Odometer → speedometer.

If you know your speed the whole trip, you can work out the distance (integral). If you know the distance at every moment, you can work out the speed (derivative). Same trip, two readings.

2 — Derivatives

Ride the hill, watch the meter

The dot walks over a hill. The little bar sitting on it is the tangent — the direction it's pointing right this second. The meter reads the derivative.

height right now: 0.00
slope (derivative): 1.00

What the sign is telling you

  • Positive derivative — going up. Prices rising, fever climbing, savings growing.
  • Negative derivative — going down. Battery draining, car braking, ice melting away.
  • Zero derivative — flat, for one instant. This is the top of the hill or the bottom of the dip.

That last one is why derivatives run the world. Want the cheapest price, the strongest signal, the best angle to throw a ball, the least material for a box? Find where the derivative is zero. That's the peak or the valley.

peak or valley  ⟺  slope = 0

The power rule: bring the power down in front, then knock one off the power. A plain number has no slope at all, so it dies.

Where the rule comes from

Nobody memorised 2x out of thin air. It falls out of the squeeze you played with at the top of the page:

slope = (x+h)² − x²h
= 2xh + h²h = 2x + h
now let h → 0  ⟹  2x

That's the limits page doing the heavy lifting. A derivative is a limit — the limit of "rise over run" as the run vanishes.

3 — Integrals

Slice the area, add the slices

You know the area of a rectangle. That's all an integral uses. Cover the curvy shape with rectangles and add them up — then use more, and thinner, rectangles.

rectangles: 4
their total area: 3.750
heading toward the true area: 2.667

What you just did

With 4 fat rectangles the answer is clumsy. With 80 skinny ones it's almost exact. Push the number to infinity and "almost" becomes "exactly" — that's the integral, and it's a limit again.

∫02 x² dx = 8/3 = 2.667

Reading the symbols: the stretched-out S ∫ is just an S for "sum". dx is the sliver of width. So ∫ f(x) dx literally reads "sum of height × width".

The 0 and 2 are where you start and stop slicing. No numbers on it? Then you're being asked for the general formula instead of one number.

4 — The punchline of all of calculus

They are the same machine,
run in opposite directions

Watch one car drive. The top graph is the speedometer. The bottom graph is the odometer. The shaded area piling up on top is exactly the height climbing on the bottom.

time: 0.0 s
speed right now: 6.0 m/s
= the slope of the green curve below
distance so far: 0.0 m
= the shaded area above
the same number, twice

Differentiate, then integrate,
and you're back where you started.

distance —differentiate→ speed —integrate→ distance

This is called the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, which sounds enormous but is just that sentence. It's also the trick that makes integrals possible to actually calculate: instead of adding infinite rectangles, you ask "what would I have to differentiate to get this?" and work backwards.

You already use both

Your water bill charges the integral. The meter's spinning dial shows the derivative. Turning the tap harder changes the rate, and the total quietly piles up.

Derivative

The best price

price: ₹0   profit: ₹0

Charge too little and you earn nothing per sale; charge too much and nobody buys. The sweet spot sits exactly where the profit curve goes flat — where the derivative hits zero.

A dose of medicine

in your blood now: 0.0 mg/L

Doctors care about the peak (where the derivative is zero — is it too high?) and the shaded total (the integral — how much did the body actually absorb?). Dosing schedules are built on both.

Six more, in one line each

  • Phone battery. Percent left is the integral; "3 hours remaining" is computed from the derivative.
  • Netflix buffering. Download rate is the derivative, the buffer bar is the integral of it.
  • Weather report. "Rainfall intensity" is a rate; "42 mm fell today" is its integral.
  • Fitness app. Pace is the derivative of distance; calories burned is the integral of effort.
  • Loan. Interest rate is a derivative; the total you actually repay is an integral.
  • Speed bump. The uncomfortable jolt is the derivative of the derivative — acceleration.
6 — Doing it on paper

Forwards, then backwards

Same function, both directions. Tap through:

y = x³

Step 0 — differentiate this

Question being asked: how steep is this curve at any x?

3x³ → 3x²

Step 1 — power rule, in two moves

Bring the 3 down to the front. Knock the power down from 3 to 2. Answer: dy/dx = 3x². At x = 2 the slope is 12 — steep.

∫ 3x² dx = ?

Step 2 — now go backwards

Integrating asks: what would I have to differentiate to get 3x²? You literally just did it, so you already know.

∫ 3x² dx = x³ + C

Step 3 — undo the power rule

Add one to the power, then divide by the new power. That's the derivative's steps in reverse. But what's that + C doing there?

x³ + 5  ·  x³ − 90  ·  x³  → all give 3x²

Step 4 — why there's a "+ C"

Differentiating destroys plain numbers. So going backwards, you can't tell which number was there. Like knowing every speed on a trip but not what the odometer read when you set off. C is that unknown starting reading.

∫02 3x² dx = [x³] = 8 − 0 = 8

Step 5 — with start and stop values, C cancels

Put in the top number, put in the bottom number, subtract. The mystery constant appears twice and wipes itself out. The area under 3x² from 0 to 2 is exactly 8.

⚠ The mistakes everyone makes

forgetting + C  ·  d/dx of a number ≠ the number  ·  area below the axis counts as negative

1. Dropping the + C on an indefinite integral. It's free marks, and it's the one thing every teacher hunts for.

2. Thinking the derivative of 7 is 7. A flat line has no steepness at all, so it's 0.

3. Assuming an integral is always positive. Below the axis it subtracts — reversing your car takes distance off the trip.

8 — Keep only this

The whole subject on one card

  • A limit is where something is heading. Everything else is built on it.
  • A derivative is a rate: rise over run, with the run squeezed to nothing. Slope. Speed. "How fast right now."
  • An integral is a total: heights times slivers of width, added forever. Area. Distance. "How much altogether."
  • They undo each other — so you can hop between rate and total whenever you like.
  • The chain rule is how you handle anything stacked inside anything else along the way.
d/dx asks HOW STEEP   ·   ∫ dx asks HOW MUCH   ·   each undoes the other
Three pages, one idea: get close, don't quite land, and see what the numbers were heading toward. That's calculus.
