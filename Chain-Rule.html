1 — The whole idea

If A moves B, and B moves C…
then A moves C.

And you find out how much by multiplying. That's it. Everything below is just this same sentence, dressed up in different clothes.

3 × 2 = 6. Turn the crank once, the wheel spins six times.

You didn't need calculus for that. You already knew the chain rule. It just didn't have a name yet.

2 — Reading the scary symbols

What dy/dx actually says

It is not a fraction with a mysterious d. Read it out loud as a plain question:

The translation

dy  =  a tiny change in y
dx  =  a tiny change in x
dy/dx  =  "if x moves 1, how much does y move?"

Speed is exactly this: d(distance)/d(time) — "if time moves 1 second, how far do I go?" You've been doing derivatives since you learned km/h.

The chain rule itself

dydx = dydu × dudx

In English: how fast the end moves, per push at the start = (how fast the end moves per middle) × (how fast the middle moves per push).

The cheat: the du's look like they cancel out, like (a/b)×(b/c). That's not a proof, but it's a great memory hook.

3 — Where it hides in your day

Three chains you've stood next to

Watch the numbers move. Nothing here is a "maths problem" — it's just stuff happening.

Stone in a pond

Ripple getting bigger

radius grows: 0.0 cm
dr/dt = 2 cm per sec
dA/dr = 2πr = 0.0
area grows: 0.0 cm²/sec

The ring widens at a steady 2 cm/s, but the wet area explodes faster and faster — because the bigger the circle already is, the more area each new centimetre adds.

Weekend job

Minutes → cups → cash

du/dt = 3 cups per minute
dy/du = 2 dollars per cup
dy/dt = 3 × 2 = $6 per minute

Nobody pays you per minute. You get paid per cup, and cups come per minute. You multiplied the chain without noticing.

Blowing a balloon

Breath → size → skin

air in: 30 puffs/sec (steady)
radius now: 3.0 cm
radius growing: 0.00 cm/sec

You blow at a constant rate, yet the balloon looks like it stops growing. Same air, spread over a bigger and bigger ball — the chain from air to radius gets weaker as it fills.

Four more, in one line each

  • Bike gears. Pedal speed → chainring → back wheel → how fast you fly down the road.
  • Phone battery. Screen brightness → power draw → percent lost per hour.
  • Petrol price. Price per litre → cost per km → cost of the whole trip.
  • Sunset shadow. Sun dropping → angle changing → your shadow racing across the ground.

5 — Doing it on paper

Peel it like an onion

Take y = (3x + 1)⁵. Looks horrible. It's two easy things stacked. Tap through:

y = (3x + 1)5

Step 0 — spot the two layers

There's an outside job (raise to the power 5) and an inside job (do 3x + 1 first). Two jobs = a chain.

u = 3x + 1  →  y = u5

Step 1 — name the inside u

Give the messy inside bit a nickname. Now you have two baby problems instead of one ugly one.

y = u5  →  dy/du = 5u4

Step 2 — differentiate the outside

Power rule: bring the 5 down, drop the power by one. Pretend u is just a letter like x.

u = 3x + 1  →  du/dx = 3

Step 3 — differentiate the inside

The 3x gives 3. The +1 never changes, so it gives nothing. This little 3 is the bit everybody forgets.

5u4 × 3 = 15u4

Step 4 — multiply them (the chain rule!)

Exactly like the gears: outside rate × inside rate.

dy/dx = 15(3x + 1)4

Step 5 — put the real inside back

u was only a nickname, so swap 3x + 1 back in. Done. That's the answer.

1 / 6

⚠ The one mistake everyone makes

✗  5(3x+1)4     ✓  15(3x+1)4

People peel the outside and walk away, forgetting to multiply by the inside's rate. Think of the gears again: if you ignore the middle gear's ratio, your answer is off by exactly that gear. Always ask: "and what was the inside doing?"

7 — Keep only this

The four-line version

  • Find the inside. The bit tucked in brackets, under a root, or inside sin/cos/e.
  • Differentiate the outside, leaving the inside untouched.
  • Multiply by the derivative of the inside. This is the step people skip.
  • Sanity check with gears: rates along a chain always multiply.
dydx = dydu × dudx   —   outside rate × inside rate
