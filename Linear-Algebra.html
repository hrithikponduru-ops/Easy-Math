Read this first — 30 seconds

Everything here is built from three things

A number on its own. A list of numbers. A grid of numbers. That's the whole cast of characters. The eight items below are just different ways of poking at lists and grids.

The one habit to build Always read a grid as rows first, then columns. Aij means "row i, column j" — like naming a seat in a cinema: row 3, seat 2. Nearly every mistake in this whole subject is a swapped row and column.
01

Vector

A list of numbers written in a fixed order. That's it.

v = [ v₁, v₂, …, vₙ ]  ∈ ℝⁿ   (written as a tall column)

In plain words

A vector is a set of measurements kept together, always in the same order. The order is the whole point: [4, 3] is not the same as [3, 4], in the same way that "4 spoons of flour, 3 of sugar" is not the same cake as "3 of flour, 4 of sugar".

The scary bit ∈ ℝⁿ just reads as: "it's a list of n ordinary numbers." ℝ² is a list of 2 numbers, which is exactly a point on a page. ℝ³ is a point in a room.

Why people draw it as an arrow

If your list is [4, 3], you can walk 4 steps right and 3 steps up. Draw an arrow from where you started to where you ended and you've drawn the vector. The list and the arrow are the same information wearing different clothes.

Everyday example Your coffee order is a vector: [2 shots, 1 sugar, 300 ml milk]. The barista only needs the numbers because the order of the slots is agreed in advance. Change the order and you get a very odd drink.
Worked example v = [4, 3] How long is the arrow? Pythagoras on the dashed triangle: √(4² + 3²) = √(16 + 9) = √25 = 5 Add two vectors? Add matching slots: [4, 3] + [1, 2] = [4+1, 3+2] = [5, 5]
Common mix-up A vector is not "a number". You can't compare two vectors with < or >, and you can only add vectors of the same length. [4, 3] + [1, 2, 9] is meaningless — like adding a two-item shopping list to a three-item one slot by slot.
02

Dot product

Multiply the matching pairs, then add everything up. Two lists go in, one single number comes out.

aTb = Σ aibi = a1b1 + a2b2 + … + anbn

In plain words

Line the two lists up side by side. Multiply the first with the first, the second with the second, and so on down the line. Then add all those products together. One number falls out at the end.

The Σ symbol is just the word "add up" written in Greek, and i = 1 to n underneath it means "do it for every slot from the first to the last".

The little T in aTb is bookkeeping: it tips the first column on its side so the two lists face each other and can be paired off. It does not change any of the numbers.

Everyday example This is a shop receipt. Quantities [2, 5, 1] dotted with prices [£3, £2, £10] gives your total bill. Nobody thinks of a receipt as advanced maths, but the till is doing a dot product every time.

The other thing it tells you

A dot product also measures how much two lists agree. Big positive number: the two arrows point roughly the same way. Zero: they're at right angles, completely unrelated. Negative: they point opposite ways. This one fact is how recommendation engines decide two films are similar.

Worked example a = [2, 5, 1] (items in the basket) b = [3, 2, 10] (price of each item) a·b = (2×3) + (5×2) + (1×10) = 6 + 10 + 10 = 26
Common mix-up Both lists must be exactly the same length — no leftovers allowed. And the answer is a plain number, not a list. If your answer still looks like a list, you forgot to add the products together.
03

Matrix multiplication

Do a dot product for every row-meets-column pairing. Nothing new to learn — just item 02, repeated in an organised way.

(AB)ij = Σ AikBkj   ← "the cell in row i, column j is row i of A dotted with column j of B"

In plain words

To fill in the cell sitting in row 2, column 1 of the answer, you take row 2 of A and column 1 of B, and do a dot product on them. Repeat for every cell. That's the entire operation.

The letter k in the formula is just the finger that slides along the row and down the column at the same time.

The size rule

An m×n matrix times an n×p matrix gives an m×p matrix. The two inner numbers must match, because a row of A has to be exactly as long as a column of B for the pairing to work. If they don't match, the multiplication simply doesn't exist.

Everyday example Rows of A = two recipes, columns = how much flour and butter each needs. Columns of B = two shops, rows = what flour and butter cost there. Multiply and you get a small table: what each recipe costs at each shop. Four answers from one operation.
Worked example A = [1 2] B = [5 6] [3 4] [7 8] row 1 × col 1 → 1×5 + 2×7 = 19 row 1 × col 2 → 1×6 + 2×8 = 22 row 2 × col 1 → 3×5 + 4×7 = 43 row 2 × col 2 → 3×6 + 4×8 = 50
Common mix-up AB is not BA. Order changes the answer, and sometimes BA doesn't even exist. Think of it as "put on socks, then shoes" — swapping the order gives a very different result.
04

Transpose

Tip the table over onto its side. Rows become columns, columns become rows.

(AT)ij = Aji   ← "whatever was at row j column i is now at row i column j"

In plain words

Imagine the grid printed on a sheet of glass. Hold the top-left corner still and flip the sheet over along the diagonal. The numbers don't change value — they just move seats. Row 1 stands up and becomes column 1.

The formula says exactly that, in the shortest possible way: swap the two subscripts. Row-and-column becomes column-and-row.

A 2×3 grid transposes into a 3×2 grid. The shape turns on its side too.

Everyday example A spreadsheet with months down the side and products across the top. Your boss wants it the other way round. You click "transpose" and the same numbers reappear rotated. That button is AT.

Why it exists at all

Mostly to make shapes fit. Remember aTb from item 02? The T was there to turn a standing column into a lying-down row so the two lists could face each other. Transposing is the standard way of getting two matrices to line up before multiplying them.

Worked example A = [1 2 3] Aᵀ = [1 4] [4 5 6] [2 5] [3 6] Check one cell: Aᵀ at row 3, column 1 = A at row 1, column 3 = 3 ✓
Common mix-up When you transpose a product, the order flips too: (AB)ᵀ = BᵀAᵀ, not AᵀBᵀ. Same as taking off shoes and socks — undoing happens in reverse order.
05

Inverse

The undo button. A⁻¹ is the matrix that cancels out whatever A did.

A−1A = AA−1 = I   ← I is the "do nothing" matrix

In plain words

With ordinary numbers, the undo of ×5 is ×⅓, because 5 × ⅓ = 1, and multiplying by 1 leaves things alone. Matrices work the same way. A⁻¹ is the "⅓" of the matrix world, and I is its "1".

I, the identity matrix, has 1s down the diagonal and 0s everywhere else. Multiply anything by I and it comes back untouched. It is the mathematical equivalent of doing nothing.

The formula is saying: undo-then-do and do-then-undo both land you exactly where you started. Order doesn't matter here, which is unusual and rather nice.

Everyday example A is a coding machine that scrambles a message. A⁻¹ is the decoder. Run your message through both and you get your original message back, letter for letter. If a decoder exists, the code is reversible.

The catch

Not every matrix has an inverse. If A throws information away — squashing everything flat — nothing can bring it back. You can tell whether this has happened by checking the determinant (item 06). If det(A) = 0, there is no inverse. Ever.

Worked example — the 2×2 shortcut A = [4 7] det(A) = 4×6 − 7×2 = 24 − 14 = 10 [2 6] Swap the diagonal, flip the sign of the other two, then divide everything by the determinant: A⁻¹ = (1/10) [ 6 −7] = [ 0.6 −0.7] [−2 4] [−0.2 0.4] Check: A⁻¹A = [1 0] [0 1] ← that's I
Common mix-up A⁻¹ does not mean "1 divided by each number in A". There is no such thing as dividing by a matrix — multiplying by the inverse is the closest thing that exists.
06

Determinant (2×2)

One number that tells you how much a matrix stretches or shrinks area.

det(A) = a11a22 − a12a21   ← down-diagonal minus up-diagonal

In plain words

Multiply the two numbers on the main diagonal (top-left × bottom-right). Multiply the other two. Subtract the second from the first. Done — that's the whole calculation for a 2×2.

But what is it for?

Think of a photocopier's zoom setting. If det(A) = 3, every shape that A touches comes out with three times the area. If det(A) = 0.5, areas are halved.

Two special readings:

det = 0 means the matrix flattens everything into a line. Area gone, information gone, and no undo is possible — which is exactly why a zero determinant means no inverse exists.

Negative det means the shape got flipped over, like a mirror image. The size of the number is still the area factor; the minus sign is just telling you it was turned inside out.

Everyday example You roll out pizza dough. If your rolling squashes a 1 cm² blob into 5 cm², the determinant of your rolling is 5. If you flatten it into a straight line, the determinant is 0 and you can't reconstruct the blob.
Worked example A = [3 1] [1 2] det(A) = (3 × 2) − (1 × 1) = 6 − 1 = 5 → areas grow 5×, and an inverse exists B = [2 4] [1 2] det(B) = (2 × 2) − (4 × 1) = 4 − 4 = 0 → everything squashed onto a line, no inverse
Common mix-up The straight vertical bars around a matrix mean "determinant", not "absolute value" and not "size of the matrix". And the subtraction runs one way only: main diagonal minus the other one.
07

Trace

Add up the numbers on the main diagonal. Ignore everything else.

tr(A) = Σ Aii = A11 + A22 + … + Ann

In plain words

Start at the top-left corner. Walk diagonally down to the bottom-right, stepping one right and one down each time. Add up every number you step on. That total is the trace.

The Aii notation means "same row number as column number" — row 1 column 1, row 2 column 2, and so on. Those are exactly the diagonal seats.

It is the easiest operation on this whole sheet. No multiplication, no flipping, no order to get wrong.

Everyday example A table of "how much each department buys from each department". The diagonal is what each department buys from itself. The trace is the total amount of internal, in-house spending — one number summarising the self-dealing.

Why anyone cares

Two lovely facts tie this sheet together: the trace equals the sum of the eigenvalues, and the determinant equals their product. So you can often sanity-check eigenvalues in your head without solving anything.

Worked example A = [2 9 4] [7 5 3] [6 1 8] tr(A) = 2 + 5 + 8 = 15 (the 9, 4, 7, 3, 6 and 1 are simply not invited)
Common mix-up Only the main diagonal counts — not the other one running bottom-left to top-right. And the trace only makes sense for square matrices; a 2×3 grid has no proper diagonal to walk down.
08

Eigenvalue equation

Most arrows get turned by a matrix. A few stubborn ones don't — they only get longer or shorter. Those are the eigenvectors.

Av = λv   ← "doing A to v is the same as just scaling v by the number λ"

In plain words

The left side, Av, is the full messy operation: rotate, stretch, skew. The right side, λv, is the laziest possible operation: leave the direction alone and just multiply the length by a number.

The equation asks: for which arrows are those two things identical? Those arrows are the eigenvectors (the surviving directions) and each one's stretch factor λ is its eigenvalue.

λ = 2 means "twice as long, same way". λ = 0.5 means "half as long". λ = −1 means "same length, pointing backwards".

Everyday example Stretch a rubber sheet sideways. A dot sitting on the horizontal centre line slides straight out along that same line — it never leaves it. A dot on a diagonal gets dragged sideways and ends up at a new angle. The horizontal line is an eigenvector of the stretch; everything else gets turned.

Why this is the big one

Eigenvectors are the directions in which a complicated system behaves simply. That's why they show up in how a bridge vibrates, how Google ranked pages, how a photo gets compressed, and how data science finds the few directions that carry most of the information.

Worked example A = [2 1] [1 2] Try v = [1, 1]: Av = [2×1 + 1×1, 1×1 + 2×1] = [3, 3] = 3 × [1,1] → same direction, 3× longer, so λ = 3 ✓ eigenvector Try u = [2, 1]: Au = [2×2 + 1×1, 1×2 + 2×1] = [5, 4] → [5,4] is not a multiple of [2,1], the angle changed → not an eigenvector Sanity check with items 06 and 07: the two eigenvalues here are 3 and 1 sum 3 + 1 = 4 = tr(A) = 2 + 2 ✓ product 3 × 1 = 3 = det(A) = 4 − 1 ✓
Common mix-up λ is a single plain number, while v is a whole list — the equation deliberately has a matrix on one side and a mere number on the other. Also, v = [0, 0] is banned: it satisfies the equation trivially and tells you nothing.

DEEP DIVE

Eigenvalues & eigenvectors

Item 08 showed you the equation. This is the part where it stops being a symbol and starts being useful.

Decoding the two words

Eigen is German for “own” or “characteristic”. So an eigenvector is a matrix's own direction — a direction that belongs to it, that it refuses to rotate. An eigenvalue is that direction's own number: how much it gets stretched.

They always come in pairs. Every eigenvector arrives with exactly one eigenvalue attached, like a name badge saying “×3”.

An n×n matrix has n eigenvalues (counted properly), so a 2×2 has two, a 3×3 has three.

The one-sentence version

A matrix usually does something complicated. Eigenvectors are the handful of directions where it does something boringly simple instead — and that is exactly why they are worth hunting for.

Everyday example Spin a globe. Every city on it moves… except the two poles, which just sit there. The axis through the poles is the eigenvector of that rotation, with eigenvalue 1 (“unchanged”). Every rotation of a ball has one, which is why a spinning object always has an axis.
Worked example — finding both pairs by hand A = [4 1] [2 3] STEP 1 — write down det(A − λI) = 0 (4−λ)(3−λ) − (1)(2) = 0 λ² − 7λ + 10 = 0 shortcut: the λ term is always −tr(A) = −7 the last term is always det(A) = 10 STEP 2 — solve it (λ − 5)(λ − 2) = 0 → λ = 5 and λ = 2 STEP 3 — for each λ, find the direction that survives λ = 5: (4−5)x + 1y = 0 → −x + y = 0 → y = x eigenvector v = [1, 1] check: A[1,1] = [4+1, 2+3] = [5,5] = 5×[1,1] ✓ λ = 2: (4−2)x + 1y = 0 → 2x + y = 0 → y = −2x eigenvector v = [1, −2] check: A[1,−2] = [4−2, 2−6] = [2,−4] = 2×[1,−2] ✓ STEP 4 — sanity check against items 06 and 07 5 + 2 = 7 = tr(A) ✓ 5 × 2 = 10 = det(A) ✓
Common mix-up An eigenvector has no fixed length. [1, 1] and [7, 7] and [−3, −3] are all the same eigenvector, because a direction is what matters. Textbooks usually shrink it to length 1 so everyone writes the same thing down.

What the number λ is telling you

The eigenvector says which way. The eigenvalue says what happens to it. Five cases cover everything.

The rule of thumb engineers actually use Apply a matrix over and over and the direction with the biggest |λ| takes over completely. |λ| > 1 anywhere means the system grows without limit. All |λ| < 1 means it settles down and dies out. That single check is how you tell a stable design from one that shakes itself apart.

Real world 1 — where a market settles

Two coffee brands. Each year, 20% of Brand A's customers defect to B, and 30% of B's come back to A. Who wins in the long run? The answer is an eigenvector.

Write the switching as a matrix. Feed it this year's market share and it gives you next year's. Do that forever and the shares stop moving — they land on the direction the matrix leaves alone.

That resting point is the eigenvector with λ = 1: apply another year of churn and nothing changes. Here it comes out at 60% / 40%, and remarkably it does not depend at all on where you started.

The second eigenvalue is 0.5, and it tells you the speed: whatever advantage your starting position gave you is cut in half every year. After five years it's essentially gone.

Same maths, bigger job Swap coffee brands for web pages and switching rates for links, and this is Google PageRank. A page's rank is one entry of the λ = 1 eigenvector of the entire web's link matrix. It is the same calculation on billions of rows.
Worked example P = [0.8 0.3] column 1 = what happens to A's customers [0.2 0.7] column 2 = what happens to B's customers Start 100% with A: [1.00, 0.00] year 1 → [0.80, 0.20] year 2 → [0.70, 0.30] year 3 → [0.65, 0.35] year 6 → [0.606, 0.394] … → [0.60, 0.40] and it stops moving Why it stops: P × [0.6, 0.4] = [0.48+0.12, 0.12+0.28] = [0.6, 0.4] → that is A v = 1·v, so λ = 1 The other eigenvalue: tr(P) = 1.5, det(P) = 0.5 λ² − 1.5λ + 0.5 = 0 → λ = 1 and λ = 0.5 → the head start halves every single year

Real world 2 — finding the story in a cloud of data

Give a computer a thousand measurements per person and it drowns. Eigenvectors point at the two or three directions that actually carry the story.

Plot height against weight for a crowd and you don't get a blob — you get a sausage, leaning. The long direction of that sausage is the first eigenvector, and it means something real: overall body size. The short direction across it is the second: build, relative to size.

Each eigenvalue measures how spread out the data is along its direction. A big eigenvalue means “lots of the story lives here”. A tiny one means “this direction is basically noise, throw it away”.

That trade — keep the big eigenvalues, bin the small ones — is called principal component analysis, and it's how a thousand columns get squashed into three without losing much.

Where you've met it Face recognition once ran on “eigenfaces”: the handful of eigenvectors that capture how faces differ. Image and audio compression, gene-expression studies, and the recommendation engines that group films by taste all lean on the same trick.

Real world 3 — why bridges have a speed limit

Anything that can wobble has a small set of shapes it prefers to wobble in. Those shapes are eigenvectors; their frequencies come from the eigenvalues.

Pluck a guitar string and it doesn't flail randomly. It settles into a few clean patterns: one big belly, then two halves, then three. These mode shapes are the eigenvectors of the string, and each has its own natural frequency — the note you hear.

Buildings, aircraft wings, engine mounts and bridges all have them too. Engineers compute the eigenvalues before anything is built, because trouble starts when an outside rhythm matches one of them: wind gusts, marching feet, a rotor at a certain rpm. The wobble then feeds itself, and that is resonance.

Why soldiers break step on bridges A column marching in time can hit a bridge's natural frequency square on. Breaking step scatters the rhythm so it never lines up with any eigenvector. The same reasoning is why tall towers carry tuned mass dampers, and why washing machines shudder violently at one particular spin speed and are fine either side of it.

Eighteen vectors

None of these are maths exercises. Every one is a list of numbers doing a real job somewhere today — and every one obeys the rules in item 01.

The only thing that changes is how many slots

A GPS fix needs 2 numbers. A colour needs 3. A photo of a handwritten digit needs 784 — one per pixel. A gene expression profile needs 20,000.

That count is the n in ℝⁿ. Nothing else about the maths changes: you still add matching slots, you still dot two lists together, you still measure length the same way. The rules learned on a 2-slot arrow carry all the way up.

Humans stop being able to picture it past 3 slots. That's a limitation of eyeballs, not of the maths, and it stops nobody.

Why big vectors matter “Similar photos”, “songs you might like” and “did this email mean the same thing” are all answered by turning the thing into a long vector and checking which other vectors point the same way.
